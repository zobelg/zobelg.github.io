Caption Studies · A short guide

Non-Speech Information

The sounds that aren’t words — music, laughter, a slamming door, a sudden silence — and the work of writing them down so that everyone gets the whole story, not just the dialogue.

Captions are not a transcript of speech. They are a description of sound. Non-speech information is the part of that description that decides whether a caption reader gets the meaning of a scene — or only half of it.

01 What non-speech information is

Non-speech information (NSI) is everything a caption conveys that is not the words people say. It is the description, usually written in [brackets], of sound that carries meaning: the score swelling under a goodbye, a phone buzzing off-screen, a character’s laugh, the wind, a gunshot, the silence after it.

The phrase is standard in captioning practice and closely related to what U.S. regulators and captioning houses call non-speech elements or sound effects. It draws the line that makes captioning a distinct craft: a transcript records speech; a caption records the soundtrack. NSI is the difference between the two.

02 Why it matters

For a Deaf or hard-of-hearing viewer, NSI is not decoration — it is information the hearing audience receives for free. Cut it, and the scene changes:

  • A character says “I’m fine” while [voice breaking]. Without the NSI, the line reads as reassurance instead of grief.
  • [ominous music] tells you the friendly stranger is a threat before he does anything. Drop it and the suspense is gone.
  • [phone buzzing] off-screen explains why a character keeps glancing at the door. Without it, their distraction looks like rudeness.
  • [silence] — deliberately marked — tells a reader the absence of sound is meant, not a captioning gap.

NSI is where captioning stops being mechanical and starts being interpretive. Someone has to decide which sounds carry meaning, how to name them, and where to place them so they land at the right moment. That decision is labor and judgment — the human core of the work, and the part machines are worst at.

03 The kinds of sound NSI carries

“Sound that isn’t words” covers a lot. In practice, NSI has to handle several distinct jobs:

  • Effects

    Sound effects

    Discrete events with meaning: [door slams], [glass shatters], [tires screech], [explosion].

  • Music

    Music & mood

    Score, songs, and tone: [tense music], [upbeat jazz], and lyrics when they matter to the story (often marked with a ♪).

  • Manner

    Manner of speech

    How a line is delivered: [whispering], [sarcastically], [shouting], [in Spanish].

  • Vocal

    Non-verbal vocals

    Sounds people make that aren’t words: [laughter], [sighs], [groaning], [crowd cheering].

  • Ambient

    Ambient & off-screen

    The world around the frame: [rain falling], [traffic in distance], [footsteps approaching].

  • Absence

    Silence & its meaning

    Marked deliberately so a gap reads as intention: [silence], [music stops], [no audio].

Speaker identification — [Ana] or [man on radio] — is a close cousin. It isn’t sound, but like NSI it lives in brackets and answers a question the hearing audience never has to ask: who is making this sound, and where?

04 How NSI is written

There is no single global standard, but the conventions caption readers rely on are consistent:

  • Brackets or parentheses. NSI is set off from dialogue — most often in [square brackets], sometimes (parentheses) — so a reader instantly knows this is sound, not speech.
  • Describe, don’t narrate. Good NSI names the sound and its meaning as economically as possible. [ominous music] beats [slow string music that makes you feel worried].
  • Place it in time. A sound effect has to appear when the sound happens. [door slams] two seconds late is a different scene.
  • Only what matters. Not every sound is captioned — only the ones that carry meaning or that a hearing viewer would clearly register. Over-captioning is as much a failure as under-captioning.
  • Consistency. The same sound gets the same description across a program, so readers can build expectations.

Every one of these is a judgment call. Which sounds matter? How much detail? Present tense or gerund? That judgment is why skilled captioners are writers, not typists — and why NSI is where the craft is most visible.

05 Where NSI fails: auto-captions

Automatic speech recognition (ASR) is built to do one thing: turn speech into text. It is, almost by definition, deaf to everything else. That’s why auto-generated captions so often strip a soundtrack down to its dialogue and lose the rest.

  • Music vanishes. ASR either ignores a score or mistranscribes lyrics as garbled dialogue. The mood NSI would have carried is simply gone.
  • Sound effects vanish. A slamming door, a gunshot, a ringing phone — none of it is speech, so none of it is captioned.
  • Manner and tone vanish. ASR captures what is said, not how. Sarcasm, whispering, and a breaking voice all read flat.
  • Silence is unmarked. A machine can’t tell a meaningful pause from a stretch it failed to transcribe.

The result is captions that look complete and aren’t. This is why “the video is auto-captioned” is not the same as “the video is accessible” — and why NSI is a useful test for whether a caption track was made by someone who was listening. Machines transcribe speech; people caption sound.
